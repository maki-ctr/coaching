Module 6. Developing players.

Team results matter, but the B course also asks how a coach develops individual players. The main tool is an individual development plan, agreed with the player, built into team training, and reviewed on a fixed date.

A common structure is the four-corner model: technical, tactical, physical and psychosocial. Psychosocial covers confidence, communication, resilience and relationships.

Here is the plan for Marcus, Harbor FC's sixteen-year-old central midfielder. Technical: his strength is a clean first touch under pressure, and his target is to receive on the half-turn more often. Tactical: he reads danger well, and his target is to scan twice before receiving in midfield. Physical: he has good endurance, and his target is repeated-sprint ability. Psychosocial: he is quiet in the group, and his target is to organise the players around him.

The targets are built into team training rather than added as extra work. Marcus gets a scanning cue in every possession practice, a short video clip review every two weeks, and captains one small-sided game a week.

Good targets are specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound. Get better at passing is not a target. Over the next six weeks, scan twice before receiving in midfield, checked on video every two weeks, is a target. And the player should agree it in a one-to-one conversation, rather than receive it.

With teenage players, growth and maturation change what is fair to expect. Peak height velocity is the fastest point of the adolescent growth spurt. It usually comes around thirteen and a half to fourteen for boys, and around eleven and a half to twelve for girls, with wide individual variation.

Maturity differences create the relative age effect. Players born early in the selection year are over-represented in selected teams, often because they are more physically mature, not more talented. Bio-banding is one response: grouping players by maturity status rather than birth year, so early and late developers each get the right challenge.

A late-maturing player who loses most physical duels should be judged on decision-making, technique and rate of learning, not on current physical output. Those gaps often close after maturation.

Around the growth spurt, knee or heel pain is common. Growth-related conditions such as Osgood-Schlatter are a known example. The right response is to manage the training load and refer the player for assessment, not to push through it. Leo, Harbor's fifteen-year-old left back, is still growing, so his plan includes a monthly check-in on growth and soreness.

Keep health notes general, and handle them under the club's safeguarding rules.

For evidence, three player plans with different profiles, each reviewed with video, show the player development competency clearly. The review should say what changed for the player, and what the coach changed as a result.
