Module 4. Coaching the session.

A plan shows what a coach intended. Video shows what the coach actually did. In the B course, coaching behaviour is assessed, so the most useful habit a candidate can build is filming sessions and reviewing them honestly.

The app's video review tool works like this. Play back the footage, start the clock, and tap a button every time you intervene. There are seven kinds of intervention. A question, which leads a player to find the solution. An instruction, which tells the player what to do. Specific feedback, which names the cue or the decision. General praise, like good job or well done. A stoppage, where play is frozen. An in-flow cue, a short word while play continues. And a demonstration.

Here is Freddy's first logged review, from the counter-press session. Eighteen minutes of footage. He made forty interventions: nine instructions, eight pieces of general praise, seven in-flow cues, six pieces of specific feedback, five stoppages, four questions and one demonstration.

From those counts, the app works out four numbers. About two point two interventions per minute. Questions made up about twelve percent of his verbal interventions: four out of thirty-four. Specific feedback against general praise was six to eight. And there were about two point eight stoppages for every ten minutes of play.

The app raised four flags. Lots of stoppages for the time: could some be in-flow cues instead? More general praise than specific feedback: name the cue or the decision. Few questions: try one guided-discovery question at each stoppage. And instruction-heavy: mix in questions so players own the solution.

These flags are prompts for reflection, not rules. A well-timed stoppage that shows the whole group a picture can be worth more than three vague shouts. The question is always whether the coaching served the session topic.

Some principles hold up well. Use stoppages sparingly, for a pattern that keeps recurring and that the whole group needs to see. When you do stop play, keep it short: freeze, rewind, show, replay. Prefer in-flow cues when one or two players need a correction, because they protect intensity and repetitions. For a counter-pressing session, intensity is the point.

Specific feedback gives players something to repeat or change. Saying you received square-on, so your only option was back, open your hips before the ball arrives, is far more useful than saying be better on the ball. General praise feels supportive, but it teaches very little.

Questions build ownership. Guided discovery means asking questions that lead the player to the right cue, so the player builds the solution. Who presses, who covers, who balances? That is a better stoppage than a list of instructions.

Finally, score the session against a rubric. The app uses seven criteria: organisation, realism, intensity and repetitions, topic focus, intervention quality, player ownership, and adapting the practice. These are placeholders. When the course issues its official rubric, replace them, and score every filmed session against the official one.
